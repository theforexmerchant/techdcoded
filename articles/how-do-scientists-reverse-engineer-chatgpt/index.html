How Do Scientists Reverse Engineer ChatGPT?

Bibekananda Patra·9 Oct 2026·11 min read
Quick answer

Scientists reverse engineer ChatGPT using a field called mechanistic interpretability, which maps the model's internal mathematical pathways. By using techniques like Sparse Autoencoders and activation patching, researchers can trace how concepts are formed and verify how information flows through the neural network.

Key takeaways

  • Mechanistic interpretability translates complex mathematical weights into human-understandable logic.
  • The process treats neural networks like compiled computer code, decompiling them to map internal circuits.
  • Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are used to untangle "superposition," where a single neuron fires for multiple unrelated concepts.
  • Open-weight models like DeepSeek R1 are crucial because they allow researchers to directly inspect internal parameters.
  • Indian researchers at IIT Bombay and IISc use these techniques to study how multilingual models process regional languages.

Every day, millions of people in India and around the world open a browser tab to chat with an AI. Whether you are a student in Bengaluru using it to debug code, a professional in Mumbai drafting an email, or a curious user asking for regional recipes, modern Large Language Models (LLMs) feel almost magical. But how do these systems actually process our thoughts? To the average user—and even to the engineers who build them—these models often act as an inscrutable "black box." You input text, a massive web of mathematical calculations occurs, and an output appears. To lift this veil of mystery, scientists are working on a groundbreaking challenge: how to reverse engineer ChatGPT. Through a rapidly growing field called mechanistic interpretability, researchers are mapping the internal pathways of neural networks to translate complex mathematical weights into human-understandable logic.

The Black Box Problem: Why We Need to Reverse Engineer LLMs

When traditional software developers write code for an app like UPI or a mobile game, they write explicit, step-by-step instructions. If something goes wrong, they can look at the specific line of code to find the bug. Large Language Models do not work this way. Instead of hand-written rules, they consist of millions or billions of mathematical parameters—adjustable knobs and dials—that are tuned during training.

Because of this, an LLM is a "black box." We know the inputs and we see the outputs, but we do not know the exact reasoning path the model took to get there. This is why researchers want to reverse engineer ChatGPT. They want to treat the neural network like a computer scientist treats compiled binary machine code, decompiling it back into a readable, human-understandable format. By mapping these internal pathways, scientists hope to ensure AI systems are safe, predictable, and free from hidden biases.

What is Mechanistic Interpretability?

To crack open the black box, researchers have turned to a specialized subfield of artificial intelligence safety and research known as mechanistic interpretability. Think of it as the digital equivalent of neuroscience. Just as a brain surgeon might map which parts of the biological brain light up when we speak or feel happy, AI scientists use mechanistic interpretability to map how concepts, reasoning patterns, and factual associations are physically represented within an AI's architecture.

Mechanistic Interpretability

A subfield of AI safety research that aims to reverse-engineer neural networks, translating their complex mathematical weights into human-understandable algorithms.

Instead of guessing why a model made a decision, researchers look at the actual parameters and trace the flow of information. The goal is to take the complex, high-dimensional mathematical weights of the network and translate them into clear, human-understandable algorithms. This allows us to see the exact "circuits" the model uses to solve problems.

From Words to Vectors: The Evolution of Language Models

To understand how we reverse engineer these systems, we first need to look at how they evolved. Language modeling has come a long way from its early statistical foundations. In the early 1990s and 2000s, IBM's statistical models pioneered word alignment techniques for machine translation, establishing the initial groundwork for corpus-based language modeling.

By the early 2000s, researchers began moving away from traditional n-gram models to use neural networks. For context, in 2001, a state-of-the-art smoothed n-gram model using Kneser-Ney smoothing was trained on just 300 million words—a tiny fraction of the datasets used today.

The real breakthrough came between 2012 and 2013. Following the success of deep neural networks in image classification, similar architectures were adapted for language. In 2013, Tomas Mikolov introduced Word2Vec, pioneering word embeddings. This technique mapped words into continuous vector spaces where semantic relationships could be measured mathematically.

Between 2014 and 2016, researchers developed sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) models using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. Google capitalized on this in 2016, transitioning its translation service to neural machine translation (NMT), replacing old statistical phrase-based models with deep recurrent neural networks using LSTM-based encoder-decoder architectures.

Then, the landscape changed forever in 2017. At the NeurIPS conference, Google researchers introduced the transformer architecture in their landmark paper "Attention Is All You Need," based on the attention mechanism developed by Bahdanau et al. in 2014. This architecture became the foundation for all modern LLMs.

From 2018 to 2023, the industry split into encoder and decoder models. BERT, an encoder-only model introduced in 2018, became ubiquitous, though its usage declined by 2023 as decoder-only models improved. OpenAI led the decoder-only charge, introducing GPT-1 in 2018, GPT-2 in 2019, and GPT-3 in 2020.

The launch of ChatGPT in late 2022 sparked massive public attention and led to an uptick in LLM usage across computer science subfields like robotics, software engineering, and societal impact work. GPT-4 followed in 2023 with multimodal capabilities, and OpenAI o1 was released in 2024 to generate long chains of thought before answering.

Crucially, since 2022, open-weight models have gained massive popularity. Starting with BLOOM and LLaMA, and followed by Mistral 7B and Mixtral 8x7B, these models allow anyone to download and inspect their internal structures. In January 2025, DeepSeek released DeepSeek R1, a massive 671-billion-parameter open-weight model. These open-weight models are absolutely vital for interpretability research because they allow scientists to directly inspect and analyze the internal weights of the model—something that is impossible with closed-source, proprietary APIs.

Step-by-Step: How Scientists Crack the Neural Code

How do researchers actually perform the process to reverse engineer ChatGPT or other massive models? It is a highly structured, step-by-step scientific process that tracks how raw text is converted into mathematical structures and routed through the network.

Step 1: Tokenization and Text Preprocessing

Before a model can process any text, the input must be converted into numbers. A vocabulary is decided, integer indices are uniquely assigned to each entry, and an embedding is associated with the index using algorithms like byte-pair encoding (BPE) or WordPiece. Special control tokens like [MASK], [UNK], or formatting symbols (such as "Ġ" in GPT or "##" in BERT) are also mapped. This turns raw text into a sequence of numeric tokens.

Step 2: Mapping the High-Dimensional Embedding Space

Once tokenized, words are projected into a high-dimensional vector space, known as the embedding layer. In modern LLMs, this hidden dimension is massive, typically ranging from 4,096 to 12,288 dimensions. In this geometric space, semantic concepts are represented as directions or coordinates. This allows researchers to see how the model mathematically groups similar ideas, such as placing the words "king" and "queen" in similar spatial relationships.

Step 3: Tracking Information Flow through Attention Heads

Next, researchers analyze the attention mechanism, which determines how tokens pass information to one another across different layers. By mapping these attention weights, scientists can identify specific "circuits." For example, they look for "induction heads" that recognize repeating patterns. If the word "Harry" is followed by "Potter" once, the induction head learns to attend to "Potter" the next time the word "Harry" appears in the sequence.

Step 4: Decomposing Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Layers

While attention heads move information around, the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) layers act as key-value memories that store factual associations. Researchers probe these layers to find where specific facts—such as "Paris is the capital of France"—are physically stored within the network's parameters.

Step 5: Disentangling Superposition with Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)

One of the biggest hurdles in reverse engineering is a phenomenon called "superposition." This occurs when a single neuron fires for multiple, completely unrelated concepts (known as polysemanticity) because the model is compressing more concepts than it has physical neurons. To solve this, researchers train separate neural networks called Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on the internal activations of the LLM. The SAEs untangle these messy signals into millions of clean, "monosemantic" features that represent exactly one concept.

Step 6: Causal Intervention (Activation Patching)

Finally, to prove that a specific neural pathway is actually responsible for a behavior, researchers perform "activation patching." During a live run of the model, they surgically alter or freeze specific activations inside the network. If the model's final output changes exactly as predicted, it establishes a direct causal link between that specific pathway and the target concept.

The Breakthrough: Untangling Neural "Superposition"

The discovery of "superposition" was a major headache for AI safety researchers. Imagine a light switch in your house that turns on the kitchen light, but also occasionally turns on the geyser or the living room fan depending on what time of day it is. That is polysemanticity. It makes tracking down a model's logic incredibly difficult because a single neuron doesn't just represent one idea.

The introduction of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) changed everything. In a prominent industry research effort, scientists at Anthropic used SAEs to isolate a single "monosemantic" feature representing the Golden Gate Bridge inside a large language model, creating a version nicknamed "Golden Gate Claude." By artificially amplifying this feature's activation, they forced the chatbot to mention the Golden Gate Bridge in response to every single query—proving they had successfully mapped and controlled that specific concept pathway.

The Golden Gate Claude Experiment

Researchers used Sparse Autoencoders to isolate a single feature representing the Golden Gate Bridge inside an LLM. By artificially amplifying this feature, they forced the chatbot to talk about the bridge in response to every single prompt, proving they could map and control specific conceptual pathways.

By isolating these pathways, researchers can address the ongoing "stochastic parrot" debate—the question of whether language models are merely repeating statistical patterns without structure, or if they are building genuine, organized internal pathways representing real-world concepts. Mechanistic interpretability shows that structured pathways do indeed exist.

Real-World Applications: From San Francisco to Bengaluru

The techniques used to reverse engineer ChatGPT and other models are not just academic exercises; they have profound real-world implications. For instance, researchers at OpenAI used GPT-4 to automatically analyze, write explanations for, and score the activations of all 307,200 neurons in GPT-2, demonstrating how advanced models can assist in reverse-engineering older, smaller models.

This research is also incredibly important in the Indian context. Researchers at leading Indian academic institutions, such as the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Bombay and the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), use these exact interpretability techniques to study how multilingual LLMs represent Indian languages.

Because India is a multilingual nation, understanding how a model processes low-resource languages like Tamil, Telugu, or Marathi is crucial. Researchers use geometric mapping to analyze how concepts in these regional languages are mapped to the same high-dimensional geometric spaces as English concepts.

Furthermore, Indian AI startups and researchers analyze the attention heads of open-weight models to identify "translation circuits." This helps them ensure that localized public service chatbots—which might be deployed to help farmers access government schemes or help students learn in their native dialects—do not inherit western cultural biases when translating and processing regional Indian dialects.

The Bottom Line

Reverse engineering ChatGPT is one of the most complex scientific endeavors of our time. By treating neural networks not as magical black boxes but as complex, map-able digital brains, researchers are slowly bringing transparency to artificial intelligence. Through mechanistic interpretability, we are moving closer to a future where we don't just use AI—we truly understand how it works.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main goal of reverse engineering ChatGPT?

The main goal is to understand how the model processes information. By mapping its neural pathways, scientists can translate its complex mathematical weights into human-understandable logic, making the AI safer, more predictable, and easier to debug.

What is mechanistic interpretability in simple terms?

Mechanistic interpretability is like the digital equivalent of neuroscience for AI. It is a subfield of AI safety research that maps how concepts, facts, and reasoning patterns are physically represented inside a neural network's parameters.

Why are open-weight models important for this research?

Open-weight models, like DeepSeek R1 or LLaMA, allow researchers to directly access and inspect the internal mathematical weights of the network. Closed-source models like ChatGPT hide these weights behind a private API, making direct mapping impossible.

What is "superposition" in a neural network?

Superposition is a phenomenon where a single neuron fires for multiple completely unrelated concepts. This happens because the model compresses more concepts than it has physical neurons, making it difficult for researchers to trace clean pathways.

How do Indian researchers use these techniques?

Researchers at institutions like IIT Bombay and IISc use interpretability techniques to study how multilingual models represent Indian languages. This helps them identify translation circuits and prevent cultural biases in localized regional chatbots.
