How Undersea Cables Work: The Glass Fibers Powering the Internet

Bibekananda Patra·15 Nov 2026·6 min read
Quick answer

More than 99% of global intercontinental internet traffic travels across the ocean floor through hair-thin glass fibers inside submarine cables. These heavily armored lines convert digital data into pulses of light, using deep-sea optical amplifiers to send data thousands of kilometers between continents without losing strength.

Key takeaways

  • Submarine fiber optic cables carry over 99% of all intercontinental internet, data, and voice traffic.
  • Digital data is converted into pulses of light that travel through ultra-pure silica glass cores.
  • Special optical amplifiers placed every 50 to 100 kilometers boost the light signal on the ocean floor.
  • Heavy steel armor and copper tubes protect the delicate glass fibers from ship anchors and fishing trawlers.
  • India connects to this global network through major landing stations in cities like Mumbai and Chennai.

When you send a message, stream a video, or load a website hosted on another continent, you probably assume your data travels invisibly through the air via satellites. In reality, more than 99% of global intercontinental internet, data, and voice traffic travels across the ocean floor. It moves through a vast, hidden network of submarine fiber optic cables buried in the sand and resting on the deep seabed.

This global infrastructure connects continents together, allowing seamless communication across the globe. Understanding how undersea cables work requires looking past wireless illusions and examining a marvel of physics, material science, and deep-sea engineering.

From Digital Data to Pulses of Light

The journey of your internet traffic begins long before it ever reaches the ocean. Computers, phones, and data centers generate digital signals, which are fundamentally just binary code made of ones and zeros. Before this data can cross an ocean, land-based cable stations must transform these electronic signals into light.

Transmitter equipment uses precision laser diodes to flash rapid pulses of light. These flashes represent the digital data stream. Instead of copper wires carrying electrical currents—which lose energy very quickly over long distances—these light pulses are injected directly into hair-thin strands of ultra-pure silica glass.

The Physics of Total Internal Reflection

Once the light enters the glass strand, it needs a way to stay inside without leaking out. This is achieved through a fundamental physics principle known as total internal reflection.

The optical fiber features a central glass core surrounded by a transparent outer layer called cladding. The cladding is made with a lower index of refraction than the core. When light travels down the fiber at a shallow angle, it hits the boundary between the core and the cladding and bounces continuously inward.

It acts just like a microscopic light pipe or waveguide, trapping the light securely inside the glass. This allows the pulses to travel around gentle bends and over immense distances without escaping or losing their path.

Total Internal Reflection

The optical phenomenon where light traveling through a material hits a boundary at a shallow angle and reflects entirely back inside, rather than passing through. This traps light inside glass fibers so it can travel vast distances.

Surviving the Journey: Deep-Sea Engineering

A deep-sea fiber optic cable is a complex piece of engineering designed to survive extreme underwater pressure, shifting ocean floors, and human maritime activity. A typical modern deep-sea cable is about 25 mm in diameter—roughly the size of a garden hose—and weighs about 1.4 tonnes per kilometre.

The interior houses the delicate glass fibers inside a central copper tube filled with waterproof jelly or water-blocking compounds to prevent any moisture ingress. Surrounding this core is a concentric stack of protective layers, including steel strength wires for tensile support, an aluminum or copper moisture barrier, and a tough outer polyethylene jacket.

Near the coast and in shallow waters, the cabling changes significantly. It receives heavy steel wire armor to protect against commercial fishing trawlers, heavy ship anchors, and turbulent coastal currents.

Boosting Signals on the Ocean Floor

Even ultra-pure glass absorbs and scatters light over long distances due to microscopic impurities. Left unchecked, a light pulse would fade into nothingness after a few hundred kilometers. To solve this, engineers place optical amplifiers—specifically Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers (EDFAs)—directly on the ocean floor.

These inline devices are spliced into the cable at regular intervals of 50 to 100 kilometers. As the light signal passes through an amplified section, it is energized directly in optical form without needing to be converted back into electricity.

These submarine repeaters require electricity to operate. Power is supplied directly from land stations using high-voltage direct current (HVDC) generators, which send electricity down a heavy copper or aluminum conductor running right through the center of the cable itself.

Laying and Repairing Cables in the Abyss

Laying a transoceanic cable requires specialized vessels known as cable ships. Historically identified with "C.S." prefixes, these ships feature massive cable storage drums, linear cable engines, and stern sheaves designed to pay out or retrieve cable smoothly at speeds up to 800 feet per minute.

When a cable breaks due to an underwater earthquake, a submarine landslide, or a ship dropping an anchor in shallow water, repair operations begin immediately. Engineers use optical time-domain reflectometers (OTDRs) from land stations to pulse light down the fiber and measure the time it takes to reflect back, pinpointing the break down to the exact meter.

Finding and Fixing Breaks

Engineers locate cable faults from thousands of kilometers away by sending test light pulses and measuring their precise echo time, guiding specialized repair ships directly to the spot.

A cable repair ship then sails to the coordinates, drops synthetic grapple ropes with bottom-dragging hooks to catch the severed line, hauls it to the surface, strips the layers, and performs a precision fusion splice before lowering it back to the seabed.

The Evolution of Intercontinental Links

Underwater communication is much older than the internet. In the 1850s and 1860s, early pioneers laid underwater lines to carry telegraph traffic using copper wires insulated with gutta-percha, the adhesive juice of the Palaquium gutta tree.

Following early trials across the English Channel and the Black Sea, Cyrus West Field promoted the first transatlantic telegraph cable, which became operational on August 16, 1858. Though that early line failed after a month, the subsequent success of durable cables laid by the steamship SS Great Eastern in 1865 and 1866 permanently transformed global communication.

Decades later, theoretical breakthroughs in pure glass manufacturing by researchers like Charles K. Kao and George A. Hockham paved the way for modern light-based optical telecommunications, replacing heavy copper telegraph lines with hair-thin glass fibers.

Real-World Networks and Indian Landing Stations

Global connectivity relies on massive international consortia and technology companies—including Google, Meta, Microsoft, and global telecommunications firms—investing in transoceanic systems. Major underwater pathways like the FASTER, AAE-1 (Asia-Africa-Europe-1), and SEA-ME-WE (South-East Asia-Middle East-Western Europe) series connect continents together.

India plays a vital role as a regional data hub in this global web. Critical submarine cable landing stations are concentrated primarily in Mumbai (Mahim, Versova) and Chennai, connecting the subcontinent directly to Europe, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia.

Domestically, India also utilizes underwater fiber optic infrastructure, such as the Chennai-Andaman and Nicobar Islands (CANI) cable project, to deliver high-bandwidth digital connectivity to remote island territories.

The Bottom Line

The internet often feels weightless and cloud-based, but it is ultimately a physical network anchored by glass, copper, and steel. Undersea fiber optic cables represent a triumph of engineering, carrying vast amounts of data across oceans at the speed of light. Without these silent glass pathways resting in the deep sea, modern global communication, international finance, and digital connectivity as we know it would instantly grind to a halt.

Frequently asked questions

Do satellites handle most of our internet traffic instead of undersea cables?

No. More than 99% of global internet traffic travels across ocean floors via submarine cables. Satellites handle remote regions, maritime communications, and broadcasting, but they cannot match the immense speed, bandwidth, and low latency of glass fibers.

Do sharks bite and break internet cables on the ocean floor?

Not anymore. While early fiber optic cables occasionally suffered bite marks from curious deep-sea sharks attracted by electromagnetic fields, modern deep-sea cables are wrapped in heavy steel armor wire and protective metallic tape, making shark bites exceedingly rare.

What causes most breaks in undersea cables?

Human activity accounts for the vast majority of cable faults. Commercial fishing trawlers dragging heavy nets and ship anchors dropping in shallow coastal waters cause most damage. Natural disasters like underwater earthquakes and landslides account for the rest.

How do engineers find a broken cable thousands of meters underwater?

Engineers use specialized optical time-domain reflectometers from land stations to pulse light down the fiber and measure the time it takes to reflect back. This pinpoints the exact distance to the break, allowing a repair ship to drag a grapple and lift the ends.

Which continent is still not connected by a submarine cable?

As of February 2026, Antarctica remains the only continent without a direct submarine fiber optic cable link connecting it to the rest of the world's telecommunications network.
