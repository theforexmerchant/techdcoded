How Does UPI Work? The Tech Behind India's Instant Payments

TechDcoded Team·22 Sep 2026·6 min read
Quick answer

UPI is a system run by NPCI that connects every bank through one central switch. When you scan and pay, your app sends an encrypted request to NPCI, your bank verifies your PIN and debits you, and the receiver's bank credits the money — usually in 2 to 5 seconds.

Key takeaways

  • UPI is a network run by NPCI, not an app — PhonePe, Google Pay and BHIM are just front doors.
  • Money moves directly between bank accounts; it never sits inside the UPI app.
  • Every payment uses two factors: your registered phone and your UPI PIN.
  • Customers see money instantly, while banks settle with each other in batches through RBI.
  • You never need your UPI PIN to receive money — anyone asking for it is a scammer.

Every day, crores of Indians scan a QR code, type a PIN, and hear that familiar "payment successful" sound in about two seconds. It feels like magic, but behind that tap is one of the most impressive pieces of public digital infrastructure in the world. Here is exactly what happens between your phone and the shopkeeper's bank account — explained without the jargon.

What Is UPI, Really?

UPI (Unified Payments Interface) is not an app. It is a set of rules and a central switching system, run by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), that lets any bank account talk to any other bank account instantly.

Apps like PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm and BHIM are just the "front door". The money never sits inside these apps — it moves directly from your bank account to the receiver's bank account. That single idea is why UPI works across hundreds of banks and dozens of apps without anyone needing to "top up" a wallet.

UPI was launched by NPCI in 2016 and was built on top of IMPS (Immediate Payment Service), India's older 24x7 instant transfer system. What UPI added was a far simpler way to identify people and approve payments from a phone.

✕ Myth

Your money is stored inside PhonePe, Google Pay or Paytm.

✓ Fact

UPI moves money directly from your bank account to the receiver's bank account. The app is only the interface.

The Five Players Behind Every Payment

When you pay ₹50 for chai, at least five parties are involved in a fraction of a second:

Player What it does Example
Your UPI app Collects the payment request and your PIN PhonePe, Google Pay, BHIM
Payer PSP bank The bank that connects your app to UPI A partner bank of the app
NPCI UPI switch The central router that finds the receiver and passes messages Run by NPCI
Your bank (issuer) Checks your PIN and balance, then debits you Your savings account bank
Receiver's bank Credits the money to the shopkeeper The merchant's bank

Third-party apps are not allowed to connect to UPI on their own. They work through partner banks called Payment Service Providers (PSPs), which keeps the system regulated and accountable.

Step by Step: What Happens When You Scan and Pay

1. The QR code tells your app where to send money

A UPI QR code simply stores text: the receiver's UPI ID (like shopname@okbank), their name, and sometimes a fixed amount. Your camera reads it and fills in the details — no bank account number or IFSC needed.

2. You enter your UPI PIN on a secure screen

The PIN screen you see is not really the app's own screen. It comes from NPCI's secure common library built into the app. Your PIN is encrypted right on your phone, so even the app company cannot read it.

Did you know?

The UPI PIN screen inside PhonePe, Google Pay or Paytm is built by NPCI, not by the app company. That is why it looks almost the same in every UPI app.

3. The request travels to NPCI

Your app sends the encrypted request to its PSP bank, which forwards it to the NPCI UPI switch. NPCI looks up the UPI ID to find which bank and account it belongs to.

4. Your bank verifies and debits

NPCI sends the request to your bank. Your bank decrypts and checks the PIN, confirms you have enough balance, and debits your account.

5. The receiver's bank credits the money

NPCI then tells the receiver's bank to credit the merchant. A confirmation flows back along the same path — and your phone shows the green tick.

All of this usually finishes in two to five seconds, any time of the day, including Sundays and bank holidays.

Why UPI Is So Secure

UPI uses two-factor authentication on every payment:

  • Something you have: your phone, bound to your bank-registered mobile number. During setup, the app verifies your SIM by sending an SMS in the background — that's why UPI setup fails if the SIM isn't in the phone.
  • Something you know: your 4- or 6-digit UPI PIN, which you create using your debit card details (or Aadhaar-based verification with some banks).

Because the PIN is only ever needed to send money, a simple rule protects most people: you never need to enter your PIN to receive money. Scammers who send "collect requests" or ask you to "enter PIN to receive cashback" are relying on people not knowing this. NPCI has also been moving to phase out person-to-person collect requests to cut down this kind of fraud.

Never do this

Never enter your UPI PIN to receive money, a refund or "cashback". Receiving money never needs a PIN — anyone who asks is trying to steal from you.

How Does the Money Actually Settle Between Banks?

Here's a detail most people never think about. When the shopkeeper sees the credit instantly, the banks have not yet moved real money between themselves.

The customer-side credit is instant, but the interbank settlement happens in batches. NPCI calculates how much each bank owes the others over a settlement cycle, and the net amounts are settled through the banks' accounts with the Reserve Bank of India several times a day. This "instant for you, batched for banks" design is a big reason UPI can handle enormous volumes cheaply.

Why Is UPI Free for You?

For payments made from a bank account, there is no charge for the person paying and, for most merchants, no Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) either — the government has kept UPI bank-account payments to merchants at zero MDR since 2020 to push digital payments.

That raises a fair question: who pays for the servers? Banks and apps bear the cost and earn indirectly — through customer data insights (within the rules), loans, insurance, bill-payment and merchant services, and government incentive schemes for digital payments. Some charges do exist in specific cases, such as certain wallet-based merchant payments.

UPI Features You Might Not Know About

UPI keeps adding features on top of the same core rails:

  • UPI Lite: a small on-device balance for tiny payments that works without entering a PIN every time, reducing load on bank servers.
  • UPI 123PAY: lets feature-phone users (no smartphone or internet) make UPI payments using IVR calls and other methods.
  • RuPay credit cards on UPI: you can link a RuPay credit card and pay merchants by scanning a QR code.
  • UPI AutoPay: recurring payments for subscriptions like OTT, SIPs and bills, approved once with your PIN.
  • UPI abroad: UPI now works in several countries, including Singapore, the UAE, Nepal, Bhutan, Mauritius and France, through partnerships with local payment systems.

A Real Indian Example: The ₹10 Paani Puri Payment

Picture a paani puri stall in Kolkata. The owner has a printed QR code and a small soundbox that speaks every payment aloud. When you pay ₹10:

  1. Your app reads the QR and shows the stall owner's registered name — so you know it's not a fake code.
  2. You enter your PIN, NPCI routes the request, your bank debits ₹10, and the stall owner's bank credits it.
  3. The owner's bank sends a notification to the soundbox, which announces "₹10 received" — often before you've put your phone back in your pocket.

The stall owner pays no terminal cost like a card machine, has no cash to count, and builds a digital transaction history that can later help them qualify for small business loans. That is UPI's real revolution: it made accepting digital payments free and simple for even the smallest businesses.

Why UPI Sometimes Fails

Most failures happen because one of the players in the chain is slow or unavailable — usually the payer's or receiver's bank server, not UPI itself. Other common causes:

  • Daily or per-transaction limits reached (most people have a limit of ₹1 lakh per transaction, with higher limits allowed for certain categories).
  • Weak internet at the moment the request is sent.
  • Wrong PIN entered too many times, which temporarily blocks UPI.
Before you retry

If a payment shows "pending", wait a few minutes and check your bank balance before paying again. Most pending payments resolve on their own, and retrying too fast can lead to paying twice.

If money is debited but the payment fails, the amount is normally reversed automatically. Under RBI's rules for failed digital transactions, banks must reverse such UPI debits within a fixed timeline or pay compensation to the customer.

The Bottom Line

Your UPI PIN is only for sending money — never for receiving it.

UPI works because it separates the front door (apps) from the plumbing (NPCI and banks), and connects every bank through one shared switch with strong two-factor security. Your app collects the request, NPCI routes it, your bank checks your PIN and debits you, and the receiver's bank credits the money — all in a few seconds, for free. Once you understand that, the most important safety rule becomes obvious: your PIN is only for sending money — never for receiving it.

Frequently asked questions

Who owns and runs UPI?

UPI is run by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), an organisation set up by the Reserve Bank of India and the Indian Banks' Association to operate retail payment systems. Apps like PhonePe and Google Pay only provide the interface; NPCI operates the central UPI switch that connects all participating banks.

Is UPI safe to use?

Yes, UPI is designed with two-factor security: your phone is bound to your bank-registered mobile number, and every payment needs your UPI PIN, which is encrypted on your device. Most UPI frauds happen through social engineering, like tricking people into entering their PIN to 'receive' money, rather than by breaking the system itself.

Do I need to enter my UPI PIN to receive money?

No. The UPI PIN is only required to send money or approve a payment. If anyone asks you to enter your PIN to receive a refund, prize or cashback, it is a scam. Decline the request and never share your PIN or OTP with anyone.

What happens if my UPI payment fails but money is debited?

In most cases the amount is automatically reversed to your account. RBI has set timelines for banks to reverse failed digital transactions, and banks must compensate customers if they miss those timelines. If the money does not come back, raise a complaint in your UPI app or with your bank.

Why is UPI free for customers?

UPI payments from a bank account have no charge for the payer, and merchant payments from bank accounts carry zero MDR under government policy. Banks and apps earn indirectly through services like loans, insurance and merchant tools, and through government incentives meant to promote digital payments.
