How Internet Works Latency: The Truth About Data Travel

Bibekananda Patra·21 Oct 2026·6 min read
Quick answer

Data does not traverse the globe in one second; it moves in milliseconds via pulses of light inside glass fiber-optic cables. This journey involves passing through access networks, national backbones, and deep-sea submarine cables, facing physical and computational delays at every routing hop. Understanding how internet works latency helps us see why distance and physics dictate our digital speed.

Key takeaways

  • Light travels at roughly 200,000 km/s inside optical glass, which is slower than its speed in a vacuum.
  • Over 99% of intercontinental data travels through buried or undersea submarine cables, not through satellites.
  • Bandwidth and latency are independent: a wider pipe does not mean data arrives faster.
  • Every router hop, switch, and protocol inspection adds milliseconds of round-trip time.
  • Mumbai serves as a major global hub for submarine cables linking India to the rest of the world.

When you send a message or load a website, it feels instantaneous. People often casually remark that data can travel tens of thousands of kilometers around the Earth in a single second. In reality, data moves even faster than that in terms of raw physics, yet feels slower in practice due to the complex relay race it must run. Understanding how internet works latency requires looking past the screen and into the physical infrastructure buried beneath our streets and ocean floors.

The core misconception is equating "one second" with global data speed. Light actually travels at roughly 300,000 kilometers per second in a vacuum. When that light enters a glass optical fiber, it slows down to about 200,000 kilometers per second due to the refractive index of the material. If data were to travel 70,000 kilometers in a straight, unobstructed glass line, it would take approximately 0.35 seconds, or 350 milliseconds. In the real world, round-trip times for global connections usually range from 100 to 300 milliseconds. Data rarely loops the entire Earth in a single packet journey; instead, it hops across precise geographical paths.

✕ Myth

Data travels around the entire Earth in exactly one second.

✓ Fact

Real-world global round-trip times are typically 100 to 300 milliseconds, governed by the physics of light in glass and hardware routing stops.

The Step-by-Step Journey of a Data Packet

To understand why a connection feels snappy or sluggish, we have to trace a packet of data from your device all the way to a server on the other side of the planet. Data doesn't flow as a continuous stream; it is chopped up into tiny packets containing source and destination addresses.

The journey begins with encoding and transmission. Your local device converts raw information into electrical signals over Ethernet or light pulses for fiber connections. Modems and routers assign your data local addresses, adding less than one millisecond of delay. From there, the packet enters the access network, often called the "last mile." This spans technologies like fiber-to-the-home, cable lines, or 5G, adding 1 to 10 milliseconds of delay as it moves from your home to your Internet Service Provider's local exchange.

Once inside the metropolitan network, data travels via fiber rings within a city. Switches use Layer 2 and Layer 3 forwarding to guide the packet, adding a few milliseconds. Then comes the national backbone, where data sprints between major cities across massive high-capacity lines running at 100Gbps or more. Here, routers perform heavy lifting to determine the next best hop, pushing latency up by several more milliseconds.

Submarine Cables and Terrestrial Fiber Paths

When your request crosses continents, it usually hits the ocean floor. Over 99% of intercontinental data travels through submarine fiber-optic cables rather than satellites. These cables are not laid in straight lines; they skirt around tectonic fault lines and oceanic ridges to avoid natural hazards.

Submarine Cable

A specialized heavy-duty fiber-optic cable laid on the ocean floor, armored with steel and concrete to transmit high-speed data between continents.

For instance, cables connecting North America to Europe span thousands of kilometers. Because optical signals naturally weaken over such vast distances, engineers place Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers every 80 to 100 kilometers. These optical devices boost signal strength without needing to convert the light back into electrical signals. Terrestrial fiber, meanwhile, is buried in deep trenches or strung along utility poles, protected by sturdy conduits to prevent accidental damage from construction work or severe weather.

Routing Hops, Switches, and BGP Logic

As packets move across the global mesh of networks, they rely on complex routing logic. Routers do not guess the path; they consult routing tables governed by protocols like BGP, or Border Gateway Protocol. BGP acts as the postal service directory of the internet, sharing path-vector updates so every network knows how to reach every other network on Earth.

It helps to understand the difference between network hardware. Switches operate at Layer 2, forwarding data frames inside a local network based on hardware addresses at blindingly fast speeds. Routers operate at Layer 3, inspecting packet headers to direct traffic across different networks. This header inspection and lookup process introduces microseconds of delay, which compounds across dozens of routing hops.

Packets may even take completely different routes if one fiber line becomes congested or damaged. Once all packets arrive at the destination, they are reassembled into the original file, video stream, or webpage.

Bandwidth Versus Latency: Clearing Up Confusion

A very common mix-up in everyday tech discussions is confusing bandwidth with latency. People assume that upgrading to a faster internet plan will automatically make websites load instantly from across the world.

Bandwidth measures how much data can flow through a pipe per second, often measured in gigabits per second. Latency measures how long it takes for a single bit of information to travel from point A to point B. Think of bandwidth as the width of a multi-lane highway and latency as the speed limit on that highway. A wider highway lets more cars drive side-by-side, but it does not make a single car travel any faster. Only shortening the physical path or upgrading the transmission medium reduces latency.

Optimizing Your Connection

If you experience high lag during video calls or gaming, check your local Wi-Fi interference or switch to a wired Ethernet cable. Local network congestion often causes worse latency spikes than distant fiber lines.

Real-World and Indian Infrastructure Examples

India plays a massive role in global data transit, with Mumbai serving as one of the world’s largest submarine cable landing points. Over twenty international cables land in Mumbai, connecting the subcontinent directly to Europe, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia through massive conduits like the AAE-1 and SEA-ME-WE systems.

At the same time, national initiatives like BharatNet work to run fiber-optic networks to hundreds of thousands of villages across the country. On a smaller scale, high-frequency stock trading firms on the National Stock Exchange place their servers inside the same data center as the exchange—a practice known as co-location—to slash their latency down to less than one millisecond, ensuring their trades execute before competitors can blink.

"High bandwidth is like adding more lanes to a highway, but latency is the speed limit. A wider road does not make a car travel any faster."

Evolution of Global Data Speed

The speed at which we move data has transformed radically over the past half-century. In the late 1960s and 1970s, pioneering networks like ARPANET operated at modest speeds of 50 to 500 kilobits per second, with global transfers taking noticeable amounts of time.

During the 1980s and 1990s, fiber optics took over long-haul telecommunications, leading to the deployment of dedicated digital lines and early transatlantic fiber links. The 2000s brought Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing, a technology that packs multiple colors of light onto a single glass strand, instantly multiplying data capacity by a hundredfold. Today, modern networks use advanced coherent optics capable of pushing hundreds of gigabits per second across a single fiber pair, shrinking intercontinental latency to manageable figures for a hyper-connected planet.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my ping increase when I play on foreign game servers?

Ping measures round-trip time. When your data travels thousands of kilometers across physical fiber cables and passes through dozens of router hops, the sheer distance and hardware processing create a delay of hundreds of milliseconds.

Are satellite internet connections faster than fiber-optic cables?

No. While low-earth-orbit satellites offer improving speeds, light traveling through glass fiber is fundamentally faster and less prone to atmospheric interference than beaming data back and forth to space.

Can data travel faster than the speed of light?

No information or data packet can travel faster than the speed of light in a vacuum. Inside fiber-optic glass, light travels even slower at roughly two-thirds of its maximum vacuum speed.

What causes sudden latency spikes during peak evening hours?

High network congestion causes routers to queue packets while they wait their turn for transmission, increasing the processing delay and causing your overall ping to jump.

Does paying for higher internet speed reduce my ping?

Not necessarily. Higher speed plans increase your bandwidth, meaning you can download larger files at once, but they do not change the physical distance or routing path your data takes to reach a remote server.
