Why Doesn't It Exist

Why Mobile Data Is Not Unlimited: The Hidden Physics

Bibekananda Patra·22 Nov 2026·11 min read
Quick answer

Mobile internet cannot be truly unlimited because radio frequency spectrum is a strictly finite, shared physical medium governed by the laws of physics. Unlike home broadband where telecom companies can run additional fiber-optic strands underground, cell towers must divide a fixed slice of wireless airwaves among hundreds of active smartphones simultaneously.

Key takeaways

  • Wireless spectrum is non-expandable and strictly rationed by physics and government auctions.
  • Cell towers share a fixed pool of bandwidth (typically 150 to 400 Mbps per sector) across all connected users.
  • The Shannon-Hartley theorem sets a hard mathematical ceiling on data transmission over any radio channel.
  • Distance degrades wireless efficiency, forcing towers to spend more transmission time on weaker signals.
  • "Unlimited" plans use Fair Usage Policies (FUP) and speed throttling to prevent total network collapse.

Every mobile subscriber has seen the enticing advertisements: "Enjoy Truly Unlimited 4G/5G Internet." Yet, dig into the fine print of your carrier contract, and reality surfaces quickly. After you consume a daily quota of 1.5 GB or 2 GB, your blazing connection abruptly slows to a crawl, or your video streaming gets locked to a modest resolution.

This is not simply a corporate pricing strategy. The core reason why mobile data is not unlimited lies in the unforgiving laws of electromagnetic physics and wireless network engineering.

To understand why home Wi-Fi can offer hundreds of gigabytes without batting an eyelid while your smartphone cannot, we have to look inside the invisible architecture connecting your screen to the sky.

✕ Myth

Telecom providers limit mobile data solely to force users into buying expensive top-up recharges.

✓ Fact

Open airwaves have a hard physical capacity limit. Without strict data management and Fair Usage Policies, cell sites would suffer immediate congestion and network collapse.

The Illusion of "Unlimited" Data Plans

In consumer marketing, "unlimited" simply means your internet connection will not be abruptly cut off when your quota ends. Instead, operators enforce a Fair Usage Policy (FUP).

Once your usage crosses a predetermined threshold, the carrier throttles your transmission rate down to 64 kbps or 128 kbps. At these speeds, a simple text message on WhatsApp might scrape through, but loading an image, modern webpage, or video feed becomes practically impossible.

In wired broadband, scaling capacity is an engineering challenge solved with hardware: if a city demands more data, internet service providers can physically lay more fiber-optic glass cables underground. Light pulses inside a glass strand do not interfere with light pulses in an adjacent strand.

Wireless cellular communication enjoys no such luxury. It relies on the open atmosphere—a single, unshielded, and strictly shared electromagnetic medium.

How Wireless Internet Actually Travels: From Packet to Airwaves

When you tap to load a high-definition video, your phone does not simply pull data from the cloud through magic. It initiates an intricate, multi-step radio dance that happens in milliseconds.

At Step 4, the entire process encounters its sharpest engineering bottleneck: OFDMA (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access). Because the airwaves are shared, the cell tower must divide radio frequencies into narrow transmission time slots and frequency slices called resource blocks. If too many phones demand packets at the exact same millisecond, the tower runs out of slices to hand out.

OFDMA (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access)

A digital modulation scheme used in 4G and 5G that splits a single radio frequency channel into dozens of smaller sub-channels, allowing a cell tower to transmit data to multiple users simultaneously without the signals colliding.

The Physics Ceiling: The Shannon-Hartley Law

Wireless engineers do not just run up against commercial limits; they run into fundamental laws of the universe. In communication theory, the theoretical maximum data speed of any wireless channel is strictly dictated by the Shannon-Hartley theorem:

$$C = B \times \log_2(1 + S/N)$$

In this equation: * $C$ is the maximum channel capacity (in bits per second). * $B$ is the available bandwidth (in Hertz). * $S/N$ is the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (the strength of the cellular signal compared to background interference).

This mathematical formula proves that you cannot magically squeeze infinite megabits through the air. The maximum capacity has a hard, unyielding ceiling governed entirely by how much radio spectrum ($B$) you own and how clean the signal ($S/N$) is. If an operator wants to increase capacity, they must either purchase wider slices of spectrum or build towers closer together to eliminate noise. Both approaches have steep physical and financial boundaries.

The Zero-Sum Game of Radio Spectrum

Radio spectrum cannot be manufactured in a factory. The government strictly regulates, parcels, and auctions spectrum blocks in narrow Megahertz (MHz) chunks to avoid catastrophic radio interference between emergency services, aviation, satellite links, and mobile carriers.

Different radio frequencies behave differently according to the rules of physics:

Mid-band spectrum (spanning 1 GHz to 6 GHz, including 1800 MHz, 2.3 GHz, and 3.5 GHz) represents the standard compromise for daily 4G and 5G usage. Yet, because these radio blocks are narrow, a mobile operator typically holds only a 10 MHz to 20 MHz slice in any given market. This narrow physical pipe must serve an entire neighbourhood.

The Cell Tower "Party Effect": Splitting the Invisible Pie

Think of a cell tower sector like a single speaker addressing a noisy banquet hall.

A standard 4G LTE cell tower sector offers a total aggregate throughput pool of roughly 150 to 400 Mbps. That 400 Mbps is not the speed delivered to you—it is the total pie shared by every single device connected to that directional antenna at that fraction of a second.

If 10 people in an empty suburban area connect to that sector, each could comfortably stream high-bitrate video at 40 Mbps. But if 1,000 people pull out their phones simultaneously at a metro station in Bengaluru or Connaught Place in Delhi, that same 400 Mbps pool is fractured into fractions of a megabit per device.

The Problem of Distance and Signal Degradation

To make matters worse, cell towers must adapt to users who have poor reception. When you stand right next to a cell tower, your phone uses advanced modulation like 256-QAM, packing massive amounts of digital bits into every radio wave.

However, if you move deep inside a basement or three kilometres away, background noise creeps in. The tower is forced to downgrade your connection to robust, lower-order modulation like QPSK.

Under QPSK, the radio wave carries vastly fewer data bits per cycle. To deliver the same 5 MB file to a distant user, the tower must spend significantly more transmission airtime than it would for a user close by. As a result, users with weak reception unknowingly drag down the available capacity of the entire cell tower for everyone else.

The Backhaul Bottleneck: Cables Behind the Airwaves

Even when the radio link between your handset and the rooftop mast is pristine, the cell tower does not house the internet inside itself. It must transport your data packet onward to the provider’s core network via a link known as the backhaul.

In a perfect deployment, every cell tower is hooked directly to high-capacity underground fiber-optic cables. But in reality, due to the high cost and logistical friction of digging up roads, many cell towers worldwide rely on point-to-point microwave dishes mounted on the mast.

These microwave links operate over wireless frequencies as well. If the backhaul connection maxes out, the tower faces an internal data jam—it cannot evacuate incoming data packets to the wider internet, regardless of how fast your 5G radio icon says your phone is connected.

From 2G Pagers to 5G: The Historical Evolution of Mobile Capacity

The battle between wireless constraints and data consumption has raged for decades. Every time network engineers devise a clever way to transmit more bits per Hertz, consumer apps expand to swallow the new headroom.

In the late 2000s, mobile operators learned a painful lesson. When the first wave of modern app-driven smartphones landed on 3G networks, carriers sold genuine unmetered, flat-rate plans.

Within months, a tiny fraction of data-hungry subscribers overwhelmed cell towers with peer-to-peer downloading and desktop tethering. Cellular towers ground to a halt, voice calls dropped across entire cities, and carriers were forced to kill off true flat-rate contracts in favor of tiered 2 GB or 5 GB data buckets.

Modern 5G attempts to solve this with Massive MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output). By grouping dozens of miniature antennas on a single mast, the tower can use phase shifts to aim concentrated radio beams directly at your phone rather than broadcasting blindly in all directions. Yet, while beamforming multiplies efficiency, it still operates within the boundaries of licensed spectrum channels.

How Streaming and Cloud Backups Drain the Shared Pool

Our mobile data consumption habits have fundamentally shifted. In the early days of mobile internet, smartphones were largely consumption endpoints for compressed text, emails, and modest web pages.

Today, smartphones are massive media generation hubs. Equipped with advanced multi-camera sensors, mobile devices record high-bitrate 4K video and immediately sync those files to the cloud in the background.

Did you know?

Streaming just one hour of crisp 4K video consumes roughly 7 GB of data. That single hour burns through more bandwidth than an early 2000s GPRS smartphone would consume over several years of normal operation.

Because high-definition video streaming (1080p, 4K) and infinite short-form feeds represent the vast majority of all cellular internet traffic, carriers routinely deploy optimization tactics. Many cellular networks silently throttle video packets to around 1.5 Mbps, forcing video players to default to 480p resolution.

Even fixed-storage data centers hosted by cloud titans implement strict limits to stop resource exhaustion. For instance, Google Drive caps free consumer storage at 15 GB (split across Gmail, Drive, and Photos), enforces a daily file-copying limit of 750 GB, and caps individual file uploads at 5 TB. If multi-billion-dollar server farms connected by dedicated fiber require protective operational ceilings, open-air wireless cell towers certainly do.

The Indian Telecom Shift: From ₹250/GB to Fair Usage Caps

The tension between data availability and network capacity played out dramatically in the Indian telecommunications landscape.

Prior to 2016, Indian mobile internet was treated as a precious, metered commodity. Consumers routinely paid around ₹250 for a single gigabyte of 3G or 4G data. Subscribers vigilantly turned off mobile data toggles when leaving home, terrified of running up surprise balance deductions.

Everything changed with the launch of Reliance Jio, which built an all-IP, greenfield 4G-only network from scratch. By adopting modern OFDMA radio resource allocation and pairing its towers with extensive fiber infrastructure, the network dramatically drove down the cost of delivering a gigabyte.

Suddenly, millions of users across tier-2 cities, rural areas, and metros were given daily allowances of 1.5 GB to 2 GB for a fraction of the historical cost. This triggered a digital boom that fueled UPI digital payments, mobile commerce, and widespread video streaming.

Yet, even this state-of-the-art infrastructure could not abandon the laws of physics. The daily cap remained non-negotiable. If millions of active subscribers were given unthrottled, infinite access at ₹0 marginal cost, cellular sectors would experience catastrophic gridlock. The daily reset at midnight exists purely as an engineering safety valve to balance the invisible, shared pool of airwaves every 24 hours.

The Bottom Line

True, unthrottled, uncapped "unlimited" mobile internet does not exist because the electromagnetic spectrum is a strictly bounded natural resource. A cell tower cannot conjure new radio frequencies out of thin air any more than an airline can fit infinite passengers into a single airplane cabin.

Every time you connect to mobile data, you are sharing a fixed slice of radio bandwidth with every other handset in your sector. Fair Usage Policies, data caps, and speed throttles are the vital traffic lights that keep that shared radio highway from grinding to an absolute, permanent halt.

Frequently asked questions

Why can home broadband offer unlimited data, but mobile networks cannot?

Home broadband uses dedicated physical cables—primarily glass fiber optics. Telecom providers can dramatically scale wired capacity by running additional fiber strands underground. In contrast, mobile internet operates over the open air, where total radio spectrum is strictly limited and shared among all active devices within range of a cell tower sector.

What happens if a mobile operator completely removes data caps?

If a carrier removes all data caps and speed throttling, a small percentage of users running continuous high-bitrate video streams, peer-to-peer downloads, and automated cloud syncs would monopolize the tower's finite radio resource blocks. This would cause severe network congestion, massive packet loss, and service failure for nearby users.

Does 5G make truly unlimited mobile internet possible?

No. While 5G introduces technologies like Massive MIMO beamforming and accesses wider high-frequency bands (such as mmWave), it is still bound by the Shannon-Hartley theorem. Even though 5G networks can handle significantly more simultaneous connections and deliver higher peak speeds, available radio frequency bandwidth remains finite and cannot support unmetered, unthrottled usage.

Why do mobile carriers throttle video streaming to 480p on "unlimited" plans?

Video streaming accounts for the vast majority of all mobile data traffic worldwide. A 1080p stream consumes around 1.5 to 3 GB per hour, while 4K consumes roughly 7 GB. By capping video streaming speeds to around 1.5 Mbps (480p DVD quality), carriers preserve significant radio capacity on the cell tower for other subscribers.

Why does my mobile data speed drop drastically in crowded places like stadiums or train stations?

A cell tower sector shares a fixed data pool (typically 150 to 400 Mbps on 4G) across all connected devices. When thousands of people gather in one place, that fixed bandwidth is split into tiny fractions per device. Additionally, interference and distant phones with weaker signals force the tower to spend more transmission time per packet.
