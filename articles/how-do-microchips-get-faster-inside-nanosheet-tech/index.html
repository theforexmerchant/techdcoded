How Do Microchips Get Faster? Inside Nanosheet Tech

Bibekananda Patra·4 Oct 2026·8 min read
Quick answer

Microchips get faster by packing more microscopic switches called transistors onto silicon. When traditional 3D transistor designs hit physical limits, engineers switched to Gate-All-Around (GAA) nanosheets, which wrap the control gate on all four sides to stop power leaks and boost speed.

Key takeaways

  • Transistors act as tiny electronic switches that control electrical current to represent binary data.
  • Traditional FinFET designs hit a physical wall below 5nm due to current leakage and structural instability.
  • Nanosheet transistors stack horizontal silicon strips so the gate surrounds the channel completely on four sides.
  • Global giants like Samsung, TSMC, and Intel are transitioning to nanosheet architectures for advanced chip nodes.
  • Indian semiconductor initiatives like the ISM are building domestic fabrication foundations while local design houses build advanced chips.

Have you ever wondered how your smartphone handles heavy mobile gaming, high-resolution video editing, and complex artificial intelligence tasks without melting in your hand? The secret lies deep inside the microscopic world of silicon chips. Inside every processor, billions of microscopic electronic switches turn on and off billions of times every second. But as our appetite for computing power grows, squeezing more performance out of these chips requires radically new engineering. To understand how do microchips get faster, we have to look past simple marketing names and explore the dramatic physical evolution of transistor design.

For decades, the semiconductor industry relied on shrinking standard components. But physics eventually pushes back. When engineers tried to shrink traditional transistors further, electricity began leaking out even when the switches were supposed to be turned off. This caused massive power waste and dangerous heat generation. Overcoming this hurdle required throwing out old blueprints and inventing a completely new architecture: the Gate-All-Around nanosheet. Let us decode the inner workings of this breakthrough technology and see how it powers the modern digital world.

The Building Blocks: What is a Transistor?

To grasp why nanosheets are such a big deal, we first need to understand the fundamental building block of all modern microchips: the transistor. Think of a transistor as an ultra-fast, microscopic electronic switch. It controls the flow of electrical current to represent binary data, using 1s and 10s to process everything from a simple WhatsApp message to complex cloud computing.

Transistor

The fundamental building block of modern microchips, acting as an electronic switch that controls the flow of electrical current to represent binary data (1s and 0s).

Every basic transistor has three main terminals and a pathway. The source is where charge carriers like electrons enter the channel. The drain is where they leave. The channel is the physical pathway between the source and the drain through which current flows. Finally, the gate acts as the controller that turns the transistor "on" or "off" by applying a voltage to allow or block current flow through the channel.

For a long time, these transistors were completely flat. This 2D design, known as a Planar FET (Field-Effect Transistor), worked wonderfully when transistors were large. However, as manufacturers shrank gate lengths below 20 nanometers, a stubborn physical problem emerged known as short-channel effects.

The FinFET Era and the 5nm Wall

By the early 2010s, planar transistors were reaching their absolute limit. Because the flat gate lost its grip on the channel, electrons leaked right through the middle even when the switch was turned "off." To solve this leakage crisis, the industry turned to a clever 3D idea: the FinFET (Fin Field-Effect Transistor).

Instead of keeping the channel flat on the silicon surface, engineers raised it into a thin, vertical "fin" that resembled a fish fin. The gate then wrapped around three sides of this fin—the top, left, and right. This three-sided control gave engineers much tighter command over the flowing electrons, drastically reducing leakage and allowing the industry to shrink chips down to the 5nm and 3nm nodes.

However, every engineering solution creates new boundaries. As manufacturers pushed past the 5nm milestone, they hit what engineers call the "FinFET wall."

The FinFET Wall

As transistors scaled below 5nm, vertical fins had to be placed closer and closer together. They became too tall and narrow, making them physically unstable and difficult to manufacture uniformly across a billion-transistor chip.

With these ultra-narrow fins, the gate lost its ability to effectively control the bottom portion of the vertical structure. Unwanted current leakage started creeping back up, threatening to stall the relentless march of computing progress. Engineers needed a way to surround the channel completely.

Enter the Nanosheet: Gate-All-Around Tech

To bypass the physical limits of FinFETs, semiconductor innovators made a bold geometrical shift. They turned the vertical fin on its side and split it into multiple stacked, horizontal sheets. This architecture is known as a Gate-All-Around (GAA) Nanosheet FET.

By running the gate material underneath, above, and between these suspended silicon sheets, the gate achieves 360-degree contact with the channel. This total enclosure cuts off the escape routes for stray electrons, entirely eliminating the leakage path at the bottom of the channel.

Did you know?

Despite the sci-fi name, nanosheet transistors still rely on standard silicon chemistry. The term "nano" simply refers to the microscopic physical thickness of the silicon sheets, not exotic carbon nanomaterials like graphene.

Furthermore, nanosheets introduce a massive design advantage: variable width customization. With older FinFETs, designers were restricted by the discrete number of vertical fins they could use. Nanosheets, however, allow engineers to continuously adjust the width of the sheets. Narrow sheets reduce power consumption for low-power mobile states, while wide sheets increase drive current for high-performance computing tasks.

How to Build an Atomic Bridge: Step-by-Step Mechanics

Manufacturing nanosheet transistors requires some of the most precise atomic-level engineering in human history. Let us walk through the incredible six-step recipe fabrication plants use to build these microscopic bridges.

First comes epitaxial layer growth. Alternating layers of silicon and silicon-germanium are grown atomically thin on a silicon substrate to form a superlattice. The silicon layers will become our channels, while the silicon-germanium layers act as temporary spacers.

Second is fin patterning. The stacked layers are etched vertically to form tall, narrow columns containing the alternating material inside. Third, technicians perform inner spacer formation. The outer edges of the silicon-germanium layers are slightly etched back and filled with a dielectric material to isolate the gate from the source and drain.

Fourth is the crucial channel release. A highly selective wet chemical etching process dissolves the silicon-germanium layers completely while leaving the pure silicon layers entirely untouched.

"By dissolving the temporary layers away, engineers leave behind a stack of horizontal silicon nanosheets suspended in mid-air, resembling miniature parallel bridges with empty space between them."

Fifth, through Gate-All-Around deposition, an ultra-thin insulating layer and a metal gate electrode coat the suspended sheets. Because the temporary spacers were removed, the gate material flows directly into the gaps, wrapping completely around each individual silicon nanosheet on all four sides. Finally, designers customize the widths of these sheets to balance raw performance with energy efficiency.

Global Giants and the Indian Semiconductor Ecosystem

The transition to nanosheet architecture is currently reshaping the semiconductor landscape worldwide. Major global foundries have taken distinct paths to commercialize this technology. Samsung was the first to market, commercializing GAA nanosheet technology at its 3nm node using its proprietary MBCFET architecture. TSMC maintained FinFETs for its initial 3nm generation but transitioned to nanosheet architecture for its advanced 2n process node. Meanwhile, Intel branded its version of nanosheet technology as RibbonFET, pairing it with advanced backside power delivery systems.

Closer to home, India is laying the foundation for a robust domestic semiconductor ecosystem through ambitious national policies.

The India Semiconductor Mission has catalyzed major investments across the country. For example, Tata Electronics has partnered with Taiwan's Powerchip Semiconductor Manufacturing Corporation to build India's first commercial semiconductor fabrication plant in Dholera, Gujarat.

It is worth noting that this initial domestic fab targets mature nodes like 28nm, which rely on established planar technology rather than sub-3nm nanosheets. However, advanced nanosheet chip design is already taking root in Indian academic and research institutions.

Academic Research in India

Research groups at institutions like IIT Bombay and IIT Madras actively focus on device physics, modeling, and simulation for GAA nanosheets and future stacked complementary field-effect transistors.

At the same time, government research facilities like the Semi-Conductor Laboratory in Mohali conduct vital R&D into advanced materials that support future scaling paths.

Common Misconceptions About Chip Scaling

Because semiconductor marketing departments love buzzwords, several persistent myths surround advanced microchip technology. Let us clear them up with facts.

✕ Myth

A "3nm" chip means every component on the microchip measures exactly 3 nanometers across.

✓ Fact

Node names like 3nm or 2nm are purely marketing terms for industry generations. They do not correspond to any single physical measurement on the finished silicon die.

Another common myth is that GAA nanosheets completely eliminate heat generation inside a device. While GAA drastically reduces static leakage—the power wasted when a transistor sits idle—it does not stop dynamic power consumption. Heat is still generated whenever billions of transistors switch states rapidly. Because nanosheets pack many more transistors into the exact same physical area, thermal management remains a formidable challenge for hardware engineers.

Finally, people often assume nanosheets are just smaller, compressed versions of older FinFETs. As we have seen, they represent a fundamental structural shift: moving from a vertical three-sided fin to horizontal stacked sheets with complete 360-degree gate control.

The Bottom Line

Microchips do not get faster simply by accident; they improve through relentless, atomic-level innovation. When traditional planar transistors leaked too much power, engineers invented vertical FinFETs. When those fins hit their physical limits below 5nm, innovators turned the architecture sideways into Gate-All-Around nanosheets. By wrapping the control gate on all four sides of stacked silicon bridges, this technology keeps electrons under tight control, enabling our devices to run faster while sipping less power. As global foundries scale this architecture and Indian design houses plug into the digital supply chain, nanosheets will power the next generation of computing breakthroughs.

Frequently asked questions

What does GAA stand for in chip manufacturing?

GAA stands for Gate-All-Around. It is an advanced 3D transistor architecture where the conductive control gate completely surrounds the channel on all four sides, maximizing electrostatic control and virtually eliminating unwanted current leakage.

Are nanosheet transistors made from carbon nanotubes?

No. Despite the prefix nano, nanosheet transistors are manufactured using standard silicon and silicon-germanium chemistry. The term refers strictly to the microscopic thickness of the silicon sheets, not carbon-based materials.

Why did chipmakers move away from FinFETs?

Below the 5nm milestone, vertical FinFET structures became too tall, narrow, and physically unstable. The gate lost its ability to control the bottom of the fin, causing current leakage, wasted power, and excess heat generation.

Do Indian fabrication plants build nanosheet chips?

Not yet for mass production. Current Indian fabrication initiatives, such as the upcoming plant in Dholera, focus on mature nodes like 28nm using established planar technology. However, Indian researchers and design houses are heavily involved in advanced GAA simulation and chip design.

Does Gate-All-Around technology completely stop a chip from heating up?

No. While GAA successfully reduces static power leakage when transistors sit idle, it does not prevent dynamic heat generation. Chips still warm up when billions of transistors switch state rapidly under heavy computing loads.
