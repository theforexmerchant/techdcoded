How Do CubeSats Work? The Tech Inside Mini Satellites

Bibekananda Patra·17 Oct 2026·10 min read
Quick answer

CubeSats work by packing all the essential subsystems of a conventional spacecraft—power, computing, attitude control, and radio transponders—into standardized modular blocks based on a 10 cm cube form factor known as "1U". They launch as rideshare cargo inside spring-loaded canisters, de-tumble using magnetic fields, and operate in Low Earth Orbit using miniaturized off-the-shelf electronics.

Key takeaways

  • The foundation of CubeSat design is the "1U" modular standard: a 10 cm cube weighing roughly 1.33 to 2.0 kg.
  • CubeSats deploy safely from canister systems like the P-POD using spring-loaded pushers and rails.
  • Miniaturized attitude control systems use magnetic torquers and micro-reaction wheels to orient in orbit.
  • Triple-junction solar cells and lithium-based batteries power embedded flight computers and radio systems.
  • Low Earth Orbit rideshare missions allow CubeSats to orbit every 90 to 95 minutes at altitudes of 400 to 600 km.

When we picture a satellite, most of us imagine a bus-sized machine covered in gold foil, weighing several tonnes, and costing hundreds of crores of rupees. For decades, orbital missions belonged exclusively to national space agencies with massive budgets. Today, thousands of satellites circling Earth are no larger than a standard shoebox.

These miniaturized spacecraft are known as CubeSats. Despite measuring just a few tens of centimetres across, they photograph weather systems, relay communications, monitor marine traffic, and even venture into deep space. Understanding how these tiny machines function reveals one of the most clever engineering revolutions of the modern space age.

Did you know?

The first flock of educational CubeSats launched in June 2003 from the Plesetsk Cosmodrome in Russia aboard a Eurockot launcher, proving that shoebox-sized satellites could operate reliably in orbit.

The "U" Factor: Standardizing the Spacecraft

The foundation of every CubeSat is standard modular architecture. In the late 1990s, spacecraft were bespoke machines built completely from scratch. If a subsystem failed during launch integration, it could disrupt the entire mission.

CubeSats solved this by establishing strict physical rules centered around a single building block called 1U.

1U Form Factor

A standardized unit of measurement for miniature satellites, defining a rigid cubic chassis measuring exactly 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm with a mass of roughly 1.33 to 2.0 kg.

Engineers scale this base unit along standard Cartesian axes to create larger variants:

  • 1.5U and 2U: Stretched formats used for simple science payloads and technology demonstrations.
  • 3U ($10 \times 10 \times 30\text{ cm}$): A common size, roughly the volume of a shoebox, offering enough space for dedicated radio transmitters or compact cameras.
  • 6U ($10 \times 20 \times 30\text{ cm}$): A flat, double-wide format frequently selected for complex Earth observation and interplanetary missions.
  • 12U and 16U: Larger platforms capable of housing sophisticated optical payloads, radar instruments, or micro-propulsion systems.

The structural frame is machined from aerospace-grade aluminum alloys, typically 6061-T6 or 7075-T6. The outer corner rails are specifically anodized. This chemical treatment hardens the surface and prevents cold-welding—a vacuum hazard where bare metallic faces fuse together under pressure inside the launch deployer.

The Ride to Space: Canisters and Ridesharing

Unlike large communication satellites that buy out an entire rocket, CubeSats travel as secondary or rideshare payloads. Launch vehicles like ISRO’s Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV), SpaceX’s Falcon 9, or Rocket Lab’s Electron carry a primary customer—such as a multi-tonne weather satellite—and pack dozens of CubeSats into the unused margins of the fairing.

To ensure small spacecraft do not touch, damage, or contaminate the primary cargo, they sit fully enclosed inside specialized canister deployers. The most famous early system was the P-POD (Poly-Picosatellite Orbital Deployer).

The canister houses internal guide rails and a heavy-duty coil spring. Mechanical "plunger switches" on the satellite's exterior stay pushed down against the canister walls, keeping the CubeSat completely powered off throughout launch.

What Is Inside the Box? The Core Avionics

To understand how a CubeSat works, peel away the outer aluminum skin. Traditional spacecraft use separate, bulky boxes for power, radio frequency control, and data management, linked by thick wiring harnesses. A CubeSat replaces this bulk by adopting the PC/104 computing standard or custom stacked circuit boards.

Inside, printed circuit boards (PCBs) stack directly on top of each other using standardized multi-pin pass-through connectors. This eliminates tangled cables and saves precious structural volume.

1. On-Board Computer (OBC)

The brain of the satellite manages system scheduling, runs flight software, monitors thermal sensors, and compresses payload data. CubeSat designers lean heavily on commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) components.

Rather than spending millions on custom radiation-hardened chips, an OBC often uses low-power microcontrollers (such as ARM Cortex-M0/M4 or AVR32 processors) for basic housekeeping tasks, coupled with embedded Linux microprocessors or Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) for heavy payload computations. To survive space radiation without failing, computers rely on watchdog timers and error-correcting code (ECC) memory to detect and fix bit flips caused by cosmic rays.

2. Electrical Power System (EPS)

A CubeSat generates electricity through high-efficiency triple-junction gallium arsenide (GaAs) solar cells. While domestic rooftop silicon panels achieve around 18–20% efficiency, these space-grade cells convert 30–32% of sunlight into electrical energy.

  • On a 1U or 3U craft, body-mounted panels generate between 2W and 15W of power.
  • On a 6U or 12U bus with unfolding solar wings, arrays deliver 40W to 100W.

Because CubeSats spend roughly a third of their 90-to-95-minute orbit in the cold shadow of Earth, the EPS routes excess energy to flight-qualified Lithium-Ion or Lithium Iron Phosphate ($LiFePO_4$) battery cells managed by specialized charge controllers.

✕ Myth

Mini satellites run on miniature nuclear batteries or chemical generators.

✓ Fact

Virtually all low Earth orbit CubeSats rely entirely on gallium arsenide solar cells and rechargeable lithium battery packs.

3. Attitude Determination and Control System (ADCS)

Once ejected from a deployer, a satellite wobbles wildly along all three rotational axes, a condition called "tumbling." If left unchecked, the spacecraft cannot point its antennas at a ground station or keep its solar panels facing the Sun.

To solve this, the ADCS uses a two-part approach:

[Sensors: Sun Sensors, Magnetometers, Star Trackers] │ ▼ [ Flight Computer / ADCS ] │ ▼ [Actuators: Magnetic Torquers & Micro-Reaction Wheels]

  • Sensors: Magnetometers read Earth's magnetic field lines, coarse photodiodes find the brightest point in the sky, and miniaturized star trackers photograph star patterns to determine orbital orientation with high precision.
  • Actuators: To stop tumbling, the computer pulses electrical current through internal copper coils called magnetic torquers. These electromagnets push against Earth’s ambient magnetic field, providing low-power torque to stabilize the craft.
  • Reaction Wheels: For pointing cameras or antennas accurately, tiny flywheels spun by brushless DC motors use the principle of conservation of angular momentum. Accelerating the wheel in one direction rotates the CubeSat chassis smoothly in the opposite direction.

Propulsion and Station-Keeping: Moving in Vacuum

Early CubeSats carried no engines at all. They drifted wherever orbital mechanics and atmospheric drag carried them. However, commercial constellations require satellites to stay spaced evenly along an orbital plane, avoid space debris, and de-orbit cleanly at the end of their operational lifespan.

Modern 3U, 6U, and larger buses incorporate miniaturized thruster packages:

  • Cold Gas Thrusters: Simple systems that vent pressurized, inert propellants like nitrogen, butane, or refrigerants through microscopic nozzles to deliver small steering impulses.
  • Micro-Resistojets and Monopropellants: Systems that electrically heat a liquid or chemically decompose a propellant to boost exhaust velocity and fuel efficiency.
  • Electric Propulsion: Miniature Hall effect thrusters, gridded ion engines, and electrospray thrusters. These systems ionize propellants and use electrostatic fields to shoot particles out at hyper-velocity speeds, producing tiny, highly efficient millinewton-level thrust over months of steady operation.

Radio Communications: Speaking to the Ground

A satellite in orbit is useless if it cannot transmit its data back to Earth. CubeSats use different radio frequency bands depending on power availability, antenna size, and data demands.

Deployable antennas use a delightfully low-tech mechanism: simple spring-steel tape measures or flexible whip wires rolled tightly inside the chassis during launch. A thin nylon monofilament holds them closed.

Once in space, the computer runs electric current through a Nichrome burn wire or thermal resistor. The wire heats up instantly, melts the nylon line, and the antenna springs open into the vacuum.

For basic orbital housekeeping—relaying battery voltages, internal circuit board temperatures, and system health—satellites broadcast in the UHF/VHF bands at modest data rates of 1.2 to 9.6 kbps.

When downlinking high-resolution orbital photography or complex scientific data, the flight computer switches to directional patch antennas operating across the S-band, X-band, or Ka-band, pushing throughput speeds from 10 Mbps to beyond 100 Mbps.

Thermal Control Without Fans

Personal computers use fans and heat sinks to move air across hot processors. In the vacuum of space, convection is impossible because there is no air to transfer heat away.

Additionally, a CubeSat orbiting at an altitude of 400 to 600 km experiences violent temperature swings. When basking in direct sunlight, exterior skins absorb fierce solar radiation; 45 minutes later, while hidden behind Earth's shadow, temperatures drop drastically.

Because active liquid cooling loops are too heavy and power-hungry, CubeSats rely entirely on passive thermal management: * Multilayer Insulation (MLI): Thin sheets of aluminized Mylar blankets retain heat inside the avionics bay during orbital eclipse. * Surface Anodization and Paints: Outer coatings reflect excess solar radiation to balance the satellite's internal equilibrium temperature. * Copper Heat Pipes: Solid copper thermal straps pull heat away from hot radio power amplifiers and route it straight out to the exterior aluminum corner rails, radiating energy directly into space as infrared light.

Real-World Applications: From Universities to ISRO Rideshares

The CubeSat framework originated as an academic training exercise. In 1999, Professor Jordi Puig-Suari of California Polytechnic State University and Professor Bob Twiggs of Stanford University created the standard so graduate students could design, build, and fly a functional satellite within a typical one-to-two-year degree timeline.

Today, CubeSats handle mission-critical tasks once reserved for billion-dollar hardware. In India, ISRO has repeatedly demonstrated this capability on the PSLV, deploying dozens of international commercial and research CubeSats simultaneously alongside primary remote sensing satellites like Cartosat.

Private Indian aerospace startups and university engineering teams now routinely design 1U to 3U payloads to test spaceborne Internet of Things (IoT) sensors, evaluate optical cameras, and monitor agricultural activity across the subcontinent.

The ultimate validation of this mini architecture arrived in 2018 with NASA's MarCO (Mars Cube One) mission. Two identical 6U CubeSats, MarCO-A and MarCO-B, hitched a ride to Mars alongside the InSight lander. Flying independently through deep space, both mini satellites relayed real-time descent and landing telemetry back to ground stations on Earth, proving that the 10-centimetre standard is capable of interplanetary exploration.

Orbital Debris and Lifespan Limits

Because most CubeSats fly in Low Earth Orbit (between 400 and 600 km), residual atmospheric drag pulls them downward over an operational lifespan lasting from 6 months to 5 years. Space safety guidelines require decommissioned mini satellites to de-orbit and burn up harmlessly in the upper atmosphere rather than remaining as space junk.

The Bottom Line

CubeSats demonstrate how standardizing a physical form factor can transform an entire industry. By confining complex electronics, power management, attitude thrusters, and radios into a modular frame based on a 10 cm cube, space access has shifted away from monolithic, multi-tonne machines toward agile, distributed constellations in low Earth orbit. Understanding the architecture inside these shoebox-sized satellites reveals that space exploration is no longer defined by how large a rocket you can build, but by how intelligently you can engineer the silicon inside.

Frequently asked questions

What is the standard size and weight of a 1U CubeSat?

A basic 1U CubeSat measures exactly 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm and originally had a mass limit of 1.33 kg. Modern design standards permit weights up to approximately 1.5 to 2.0 kg per unit, making them compact enough to hold in your hands.

How do CubeSats get launched into space?

CubeSats launch as rideshare or secondary payloads aboard commercial rockets like SpaceX Falcon 9, Rocket Lab Electron, or ISRO's PSLV. They sit inside secure canister deployers and are pushed into orbit by a mechanical spring once the main payload clears.

How do CubeSats generate electrical power in orbit?

CubeSats use high-efficiency triple-junction gallium arsenide solar cells mounted on their external chassis or deployable wings. These cells deliver 30% to 32% efficiency, generating 2W to 100W depending on satellite size, and charge onboard lithium-ion or lithium iron phosphate batteries.

How do CubeSats control their orientation without gravity?

CubeSats use an Attitude Determination and Control System (ADCS). Sensors like magnetometers, star trackers, and sun sensors detect orientation, while actuators like magnetic torque coils push against Earth’s magnetic field to de-tumble, and micro-reaction wheels spin to provide precise pointing.

How long can a CubeSat stay operational in space?

In Low Earth Orbit (400 to 600 km), CubeSats typically remain operational between 6 months and 5 years. Faint atmospheric drag gradually slows the satellite down, causing it to fall back into Earth's atmosphere, where it burns up safely.
