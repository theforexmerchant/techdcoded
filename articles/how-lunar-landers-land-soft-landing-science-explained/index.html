How Lunar Landers Land: Soft Landing Science Explained

Bibekananda Patra·23 Nov 2026·11 min read
Quick answer

Because the Moon has no atmosphere, spacecraft cannot use parachutes or airbrakes to slow down. Instead, lunar landers execute a soft landing through fully autonomous retro-rocket burns, real-time LIDAR terrain mapping, and crushable shock-absorbing landing gear.

Key takeaways

  • The Moon's lack of atmosphere forces landers to rely 100% on variable-thrust retro-rockets for deceleration.
  • Signal delays of ~1.3 seconds mean landing maneuvers must be completely autonomous using onboard GNC algorithms.
  • Cameras and Doppler LIDAR map terrain in real time to avoid craters, steep slopes, and boulders.
  • Main engines cut off at 1 to 3 meters height to prevent rocket plumes from stirring up destructive lunar dust.
  • Landing gear legs contain crushable honeycomb structures that absorb final impact forces upon touchdown.

When India's Chandrayaan-3 spacecraft safely touched down near the Moon's south pole on August 23, 2023, millions of people watched the telemetry stream in awe. But stepping back from the celebration reveals a daunting engineering reality: pulling off a soft landing on another celestial body is one of the hardest technical challenges in aerospace engineering. The spacecraft hurtles through space at over 6,000 kilometers per hour, carrying fragile scientific instruments, sensors, and computer hardware. To survive touchdown, it must reduce that velocity to near zero—hitting the ground no faster than a gentle walking speed.

If a spacecraft hits the surface hard without slowing down, it is known as an impact probe—a mission designed for a high-speed collision, like the Soviet Union's historic Luna 2 in 1959. But to deploy rovers, measure moonquakes, or collect soil samples, engineers must build a soft lander: a craft engineered to decelerate gracefully and remain fully operational after touchdown.

Here is the complete engineering breakdown of how lunar landers land softly on the Moon, from orbital physics to autonomous guidance software.

Soft Landing vs Impact Landing

An impact landing (hard landing) deliberately or unavoidably destroys the spacecraft upon surface contact. A soft landing uses controlled deceleration systems to bring the craft to an intact halt, preserving onboard instruments.

Atmospheric Braking vs. Powered Soft Landings

To understand how a spacecraft lands on the Moon, it helps to first understand why landing on Earth, Mars, or Venus relies on a completely different set of physical tools.

When a lander enters the atmosphere of Earth, Venus, Mars, or Saturn's moon Titan, atmospheric friction works to the spacecraft's advantage. Mission designers equip the vehicle with heat shields, aerodynamic airbrakes, and massive parachutes. As the craft punches through atmospheric gases, air drag naturally converts kinetic energy into heat, scrubbing off the vast majority of its entry velocity long before touchdown thrusters ignite.

The Moon has zero atmosphere. There is no air to cushion a fall, no wind to inflate a parachute, and no atmospheric friction to create drag. On the Moon, lunar gravity accelerates objects downward at approximately 1.62 m/s²—roughly one-sixth of Earth's surface gravity—with nothing in the environment to resist that acceleration.

Because of this vacuum, a lunar lander must supply its own deceleration force. A soft landing must be accomplished entirely through powered retro-rocket thrust. Every single kilometer per hour of orbital speed must be cancelled out by burning chemical propellants directly opposing the direction of flight.

Did you know?

Because the Moon lacks an atmosphere, parachutes cannot function at all. Every single unit of deceleration force must be generated by burning onboard rocket fuel against lunar gravity.

The Step-by-Step Lunar Soft Landing Sequence

Landing on the Moon is not a single braking event. It is an intricately choreographed sequence of flight maneuvers executed over thousands of kilometers.

Phase 1: Deorbit Burn & Transfer Orbit

The descent sequence begins while the lander is orbiting in a low circular path around the Moon, typically at an altitude of around 100 km, travelling horizontally at roughly 1.68 km/s (approx. 6,000 km/h). The main descent thrusters execute a brief deorbit burn—firing against the spacecraft's direction of motion to drop its lowest orbital point (perilune) to about 10 to 15 km directly above the intended target area.

Phase 2: Main Braking Phase

As the lander reaches perilune, the main retro-rocket engine ignites for its longest continuous firing. Aimed continuously opposite to the spacecraft's horizontal velocity vector, this burn destroys over 90% of the spacecraft’s kinetic energy. Over several minutes, the lander descends from 15 km down to roughly 2 to 3 km altitude, while its horizontal velocity drops from 1.7 km/s down to subsonic speeds.

Phase 3: Pitch-Up & Approach Phase

At an altitude of roughly 2 to 3 km, the guidance system commands auxiliary thrusters to tilt (pitch up) the lander into an upright orientation. Pitching upright directs rocket thrust straight downward to oppose lunar gravity while giving onboard optical cameras, laser sensors, and radars a clear, unobstructed view of the ground below.

Phase 4: Hazard Detection & Avoidance Phase

While descending between 1 km and 100 meters, high-speed optical cameras, laser altimeters, and LIDAR systems rapidly map the local terrain. Onboard autonomous guidance algorithms identify surface hazards such as steep slopes, deep craters, and large boulders. If the primary target spot is dangerous, the software dynamically recalculates flight trajectories to steer toward a flat, safe landing zone nearby.

Phase 5: Terminal Vertical Descent & Touchdown

In the final 100 meters, the lander transitions into a purely vertical descent at low speed (typically 1 to 2 m/s) directly above the chosen safe spot. Modern landers throttle down or shut off their retro-rockets slightly before footpad contact (at 1 to 3 meters altitude). The lander drops the final distance under lunar gravity, absorbing the remaining shock via crushable structures inside its landing gear legs.

The Engineering Behind Retro-Rockets and Propulsion

At the core of every lunar soft lander sits a specialized propulsion system. Unlike launcher engines that burn at full blast until fuel exhausts, lunar landing engines require precise, variable-thrust liquid propellant engines capable of dynamic throttling.

As the lander burns through hundreds of kilograms of liquid propellant during descent, the vehicle's total mass drops significantly. To maintain stable control without shooting back upward or slamming into the ground, the flight computer must dynamically adjust thrust output continuously to match the changing mass of the craft.

+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+ | LUNAR LANDER PROPULSION SYSTEMS | +------------------------------------+----------------------------------+ | Main Variable-Thrust Rocket Engine | Reaction Control System (RCS) | +------------------------------------+----------------------------------+ | Throttles dynamically as fuel | Small auxiliary cold-gas or jet | | burns off; provides main deceleration| nozzles pulsed to control pitch, | | force against orbital velocity. | roll, yaw, and lateral position. | +------------------------------------+----------------------------------+

To control spacecraft orientation (pitch, roll, and yaw), landers rely on a Reaction Control System (RCS). RCS thrusters are small auxiliary jets that pulse short bursts of gas to keep the craft balanced. While the main retro-rocket provides the massive muscle to slow down, RCS thrusters provide the fine balance required to remain stable.

Running out of propellant prior to touchdown or firing thrusters with incorrect orientation results in catastrophic mission failure.

The Fuel Constraint

Once a lander begins its main braking phase, it cannot abort back to orbit if propellant runs low. Running out of fuel even a few seconds before touchdown guarantees a fatal high-speed surface impact.

Onboard Brains: Autonomous Guidance and Control

One of the most surprising aspects of moon landings is that nobody on Earth is steering the lander with a joystick.

Radio signals traveling between Earth and the Moon take approximately 1.3 seconds each way. A round-trip communication loop takes over 2.6 seconds. At terminal descent velocities, a 2.6-second delay means that by the time an engineer on Earth sees a crater and sends a correction command, the lander has already crashed.

Because real-time manual remote control from Earth is physically impossible, lunar soft landings must be 100% fully autonomous.

Guidance & Navigation Component Operational Function During Soft Landing
Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) Measure orientation shifts, accelerations, and spin rates hundreds of times per second.
Extended Kalman Filters Sensor-fusion software algorithms that clean noisy data to estimate exact height and speed.
Fault-Tolerant Flight Computer Executes Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) code to adjust engine valves autonomously.
Doppler LIDAR Fires laser pulses downward to measure exact 3D velocity relative to the lunar surface.

The lander's flight computer processes streams of inputs from accelerometers, gyroscopes, and radar. Flight software runs recursive filtering algorithms (such as extended Kalman filters) to estimate the spacecraft's exact position, speed, and attitude hundreds of times per second, translating calculations into immediate thrust commands.

"Because speed-of-light communication delays make remote piloting impossible, a lunar lander is an autonomous robot solving millions of calculus equations per second to save itself from crashing."

Hazard Detection: How Landers 'See' Craters and Boulders

Early robotic probes, such as the Soviet Union's Luna 9 (1966) or NASA's Surveyor program (1966–1968), aimed for broad, flat lunar maria and hoped for good terrain. Modern missions target scientifically rich areas—such as the lunar south pole—where steep craters and boulders dominate.

To safely land in complex terrain, modern spacecraft rely on integrated sensing hardware:

  • Terrain Relative Navigation (TRN): Downward cameras capture high-resolution images during descent. Onboard vision algorithms compare crater distributions against pre-loaded satellite orbital maps, determining absolute coordinates with high precision.
  • Laser Altimeters & Doppler LIDAR: Laser altimeters measure exact altitude above ground level, while Doppler LIDAR tracks 3D ground relative velocity to eliminate sideways drift.
  • Optical Hazard Cameras: High-frame-rate visual cameras feed terrain streams into computer vision software. The system detects surface roughness, rocks, or steep gradients, commanding trajectory shifts to avoid obstacles.

India's Path to the Moon: Chandrayaan-2 vs Chandrayaan-3

India's lunar exploration program offers a real-world look at how landing technology evolves through flight experience.

In September 2019, ISRO attempted a soft landing with the Vikram lander during the Chandrayaan-2 mission, targeting the lunar south polar region. During the fine braking phase, a software anomaly in the control guidance system caused thrust deviations beyond what the system could handle. The lander lost control balance and crashed.

ISRO engineers redesigned the system for Chandrayaan-3 (2023) by implementing key technical improvements:

  1. Software Margin Expansion: Flight algorithms were reprogrammed to handle wider velocity deviations, engine variations, and sensor errors.
  2. Reinforced Physical Hardware: Landing legs were strengthened to absorb higher impact velocities, and propellant tank capacity was increased.
  3. Sensor Redundancy: Engineers added a dedicated Laser Doppler Velocimeter alongside upgraded hazard-detection software.

On August 23, 2023, Chandrayaan-3's Vikram lander successfully soft-landed near crater Manzinus U in the lunar south polar region. The accomplishment made India the fourth nation to complete a lunar soft landing, and the first country in history to land near the lunar south pole.

Global exploration continues to expand. China's Chang'e 5 (2020) and Chang'e 6 (2024) achieved soft landings to return soil samples from the Moon's near and far sides. Under NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program, private firms like Intuitive Machines (whose Nova-C Odysseus lander launched on a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket and landed on February 22, 2024) are developing landers to scout resources and prepare for human return missions.

The Final Touchdown: Shock Absorbers and Plume Control

The final three meters of flight present a subtle aerodynamic trap.

As retro-rockets fire close to the ground, high-velocity engine exhaust hits loose lunar soil (regolith). This rocket plume surface interaction can blow abrasive dust over camera lenses or generate high-pressure shockwave rebounds that destabilize the vehicle.

To prevent plume rebound, landers throttle down or shut off main engines at 1 to 3 meters altitude.

+---------------------------------------------+ | TERMINAL TOUCHDOWN SEQUENCE | +---------------------------------------------+ | 1. Main engines cut off at 1 to 3 meters. | | 2. Plume rebound and dust clouds minimized. | | 3. Lander enters short gravity free-fall. | | 4. Honeycomb inside legs crushes on contact | | to absorb remaining kinetic energy. | +---------------------------------------------+

The lander drops the remaining distance in lunar gravity, striking the ground at roughly 1 to 2 m/s. To absorb residual energy without bouncing or tipping over, the landing gear legs contain internal crushable honeycomb structures (typically aluminum). As footpads hit the lunar regolith, the honeycomb material deforms mechanically, trapping impact shock and bringing the craft to a stable rest.

✕ Myth

Lunar landers fire their retro-rockets continuously until their footpads touch the ground.

✓ Fact

Main engines cut off 1 to 3 meters above the surface so rocket exhaust plumes do not bounce off the ground, create shockwaves, or blow abrasive dust onto instruments.

The Bottom Line

Soft-landing on the Moon is a masterclass in autonomous robotic control. Without atmospheric air to slow spacecraft down with parachutes, landers rely completely on variable-thrust retro-rockets, high-speed LIDAR sensors, and self-correcting flight software.

From historic robotic landers to modern achievements like Chandrayaan-3, every successful touchdown relies on a seamless balance of physics, dynamic throttling, and autonomous algorithms—delivering scientific payloads safely onto the lunar surface.

Frequently asked questions

Why can't lunar landers use parachutes like Mars landers do?

Parachutes require atmospheric air molecules to generate drag and counteract gravity. Because the Moon has no atmosphere, there is no air to inflate a parachute canopy or slow down a descending spacecraft. A lunar lander must rely entirely on variable-thrust retro-rockets to decelerate from orbital speed down to a gentle touchdown.

Why is manual remote control from Earth impossible during a moon landing?

Radio signals take approximately 1.3 seconds to travel between Earth and the Moon in each direction, creating a round-trip communications delay of over 2.6 seconds. Because descent guidance adjustments are required every few milliseconds, a human pilot operating a joystick from Earth would react far too slowly, causing the spacecraft to crash before commands could arrive.

What caused India's Chandrayaan-2 lander to crash in 2019?

During Chandrayaan-2's descent in September 2019, a software anomaly in the control guidance system caused thrust deviations slightly beyond what the flight algorithms were calibrated to handle. The lander lost balance during its fine braking phase and crashed. ISRO solved this in Chandrayaan-3 by upgrading flight software tolerances, expanding leg strength, and adding redundant sensors.

Why do lunar landers shut off their engines right before touching the ground?

Main descent engines cut off at an altitude of 1 to 3 meters to prevent rocket plume surface interaction. Firing engines directly against the ground generates high-pressure shockwave rebounds that can destabilize the vehicle. Shutting engines off early also prevents high-speed, abrasive lunar dust from blowing onto optical sensors, cameras, and scientific instruments.

How do crushable honeycomb shock absorbers in landing gear work?

The legs of a lunar lander contain internal metallic honeycomb structures, typically made of aluminum. When the footpads impact the lunar surface, the remaining kinetic energy forces the internal honeycomb walls to crush and deform mechanically in a controlled way. This structural deformation absorbs touchdown energy cleanly, preventing the spacecraft from bouncing or tipping over.
