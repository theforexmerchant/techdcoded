How Surgical Robots Work: Tech Inside ₹1 Cr Systems

Bibekananda Patra·24 Nov 2026·8 min read
Quick answer

Surgical robots do not operate on their own; they are real-time master-slave mechanical systems that mirror a human surgeon's hand movements. By combining 3D HD binocular vision, high-frequency tremor-filtering algorithms, and micro-actuators driving wristed tools inside 5mm to 8mm shafts, these ₹1 Cr to ₹25+ Cr systems translate large hand gestures into jitter-free, microscopic cuts inside the body.

Key takeaways

  • Surgical robots are telemanipulators; a human surgeon controls every single movement from a master console in real time.
  • Digital signal processors filter out 6–10 Hz human hand tremors and apply motion scaling to translate hand gestures into micro-movements.
  • Miniaturized instruments feature mechanical wrists offering 7 degrees of freedom inside narrow 5mm or 8mm shafts.
  • Stereo-endoscopes project high-definition 3D imagery with 10x to 15x optical magnification and real-time fluorescent tissue assessment.
  • Homegrown Indian systems like SSI Mantra lower hardware costs to ₹4–5 Crore, expanding advanced robotics beyond tier-1 hospital networks.

Picture a surgeon sitting at a comfortable control cockpit across the operating room, looking into a binocular 3D viewer. As they move their fingers inside sensory controllers, motorized mechanical arms stationed directly over the patient duplicate those exact motions inside human tissue—without a fraction of a millimeter of hand jitter.

This is not autonomous sci-fi surgery. High-precision medical platforms costing anywhere from ₹1 Crore to ₹25+ Crore operate as master-slave telemanipulation ecosystems. The computer does not decide where to cut or stitch. Instead, it reads human mechanical commands, processes them through digital algorithms, and delivers precision control inside narrow anatomical spaces. To understand how surgical robots work, we must look inside the mechanical engineering, high-speed signal processing, and specialized optics that drive these advanced medical machines.

The Three-Part Hardware Architecture

A modern surgical robot is not a single, standalone robotic arm. It is a synchronized network made up of three distinct physical units connected by high-speed data cables.

The first component is the Master Console. This is the control centre where the primary surgeon sits outside the sterile area around the patient. It features an ergonomic cockpit equipped with a 3D binocular display and dual master hand controllers. When the surgeon places their fingers into these sensory controllers, sensor arrays track position, orientation, and gripping force in real time.

The second component is the Patient-Side Cart. Positioned directly over the operating table, this multi-armed robotic unit holds the actual surgical tools. One arm holds a 3D optical endoscope (the visual camera), while two to three additional arms hold wristed micro-instruments. These arms lock onto surgical access ports inserted into the patient to perform the physical work.

The third component is the Vision Cart. Serving as the central processing hub, the vision cart contains high-performance central microprocessors, high-definition light sources, power supply units, and auxiliary video screens for the assistant surgeon, scrub nurses, and surgical team standing at the operating table.

Step-by-Step: Workflow of a Robotic Surgery

Translating a surgeon's physical hand motions into precise mechanical actions inside a patient follows a strict sequential workflow.

Before the actual operation begins, surgical teams often use Virtual Reality (VR) simulation platforms. These interactive software environments allow doctors to practice procedures risk-free and map out customized treatment strategies using a patient's 3D Computer-Aided Design (CAD) anatomical models.

In the operating room, the procedure starts when surgeons make small incisions measuring just 5 to 12 millimeters in the patient's body. Hollow sleeves called trocars are placed into these incisions to act as entry ports. The mechanical arms of the patient-side cart are then wheeled over and locked securely onto these trocars.

Once the 3D camera and wristed tools are inside the body cavity, the surgeon sits at the master console. As the surgeon moves their hands, central microprocessors sample motion coordinates thousands of times per second. The system digitizes these movements, removes natural hand tremors, scales down physical motion distance, and sends electric motor commands to the patient-side arms.

Inside Micro-Actuators: Giving Steel a Human Wrist

Conventional minimally invasive techniques (laparoscopic surgery) rely on long, rigid instruments inserted through small ports. Because these manual tools act like straight chopsticks, they offer only 4 degrees of freedom (moving up/down, left/right, in/out, and rotation). This makes it difficult to manipulate tissue around corners or in tight spaces.

Wristed robotic instruments (such as EndoWrist technology) solve this limit by providing 7 degrees of freedom. These movements include pitch, yaw, roll, grip, elevation, azimuth, and translation. This level of dexterity matches—and in some angles exceeds—the mechanical range of motion of a human hand and wrist.

To achieve 7 degrees of movement inside a narrow 5mm or 8mm shaft, engineers use miniaturized mechanical linkages driven by high-strength tungsten-braided cables. Tiny electric micro-motors located inside the upper housing of the robotic arm pull and relax these tungsten cables.

By pulling on these tiny cables, the micro-actuators convert rotation commands from the central processor into delicate joint bending at the instrument tip. This tiny wrist mechanism enables the tool to pinch, sew sutures, and dissect organ tissue in compact anatomical spaces where a surgeon's hands could never fit.

Degrees of Freedom

The number of independent ways a mechanical instrument tip can move in three-dimensional space. Standard laparoscopic tools have 4 degrees of freedom, while wristed robotic instruments offer 7 degrees.

3D HD Vision Systems: Seeing Inside the Body

Precise mechanical control requires crystal-clear visual feedback. Surgical platforms solve this by placing dual optical pathways inside a single stereo-endoscope.

The camera tube houses two separate high-definition camera chips side by side. These sensors capture two distinct video streams simultaneously—one feed for the surgeon's left eye and one for the right eye. The vision processing unit sends these synchronized streams directly to the master console display.

When viewed through the binocular viewport, the stereo feed creates a true 3D visual field with accurate spatial depth perception. The optical assembly delivers 10x to 15x magnification, allowing surgeons to inspect tiny blood vessels and nerve fibers clearly.

Did you know?

Integrated fluorescence imaging systems (such as Firefly technology) use specialized Indocyanine Green (ICG) dye injected into the patient. Under light emitted by the endoscope, blood flow and organ perfusion glow bright green, helping surgeons instantly identify tissue health during complex cuts.

Tremor-Filtering Algorithms and Motion Scaling

Human hands naturally experience microscopic muscle vibrations known as physiological hand tremors. These natural hand tremors happen within a frequency band of 6 Hz to 10 Hz. Under high visual magnification, even tiny hand vibrations can make precise surgical cuts difficult.

Robotic surgery platforms eliminate this problem using Active Digital Signal Processing (DSP). As the surgeon operates the hand controllers, central processors continuously sample hand coordinates thousands of times per second. Algorithms isolate the 6–10 Hz frequency spectrum and delete these high-frequency vibrations from the digital control signal before sending commands to the patient-side cart.

Motion Scaling

An algorithmic feature that scales down physical hand movements. For instance, with a 5:1 motion scaling setting, a 5-centimeter movement of the surgeon's hand at the console translates into a precise 1-centimeter movement by the instrument tip inside the patient.

By combining active tremor filtering with motion scaling, the computer system ensures steady, jitter-free movements. If a surgeon's hand trembles slightly due to fatigue, the DSP software smooths out the command, helping protect delicate nearby tissues.

From Military Research to Indian Hospitals

The telemanipulating technology behind modern medical platforms did not begin in civilian operating rooms. In the late 1980s and 1990s, military defense agencies like DARPA funded research into remote tele-surgery. The initial goal was to build machines that would allow army surgeons to operate on wounded soldiers remotely from safe locations.

While true remote field operations were limited by network signal latency, engineers realized the tele-surgery platform was ideal for civilian minimally invasive procedures. This led to commercial master-slave systems like Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci platform, which gained US FDA clearing in 2000 and established the standard for modern robotic-assisted minimally invasive surgery (RAMIS).

Today, multi-arm surgical platforms are widely deployed across major medical centers in India—including AIIMS, Apollo Hospitals, Max Healthcare, Fortis Healthcare, and Medanta. Indian surgical teams use these systems for radical prostatectomies, renal transplants, and complex cardiac repairs.

Indigenous Indian Hardware Innovation

While imported surgical platforms cost upwards of ₹20+ Crore, Indian engineering is lowering costs. Homegrown systems like SSI Mantra (developed by SS Innovations) have entered the market at approximately ₹4 to ₹5 Crore. By reducing capital costs, local manufacturing aims to make precision robotic surgery available across tier-2 and tier-3 Indian cities.

Common Myths vs. Factual Realities

Despite their growing use, surgical robots are frequently misunderstood due to popular media tropes.

✕ Myth

Surgical robots perform operations on their own using AI.

✓ Fact

Surgical platforms are master-slave telemanipulators. Every movement, cut, and stitch is actively controlled in real time by an attending human surgeon sitting at the master console.

Another common belief is that robotic platforms are intended to replace medical teams. In reality, these systems act purely as instrument enhancers. They provide 3D visual clarity, wrist flexibility, and motion stabilization, but they still require an entire team of trained doctors, nurses, and assistants present in the operating room.

Additionally, many assume robotic tools can be used indefinitely. In reality, most micro-instruments contain embedded microchips that track mechanical cycles. To guarantee structural safety and prevent cable failure, the internal processor automatically locks the instrument after a fixed usage limit (typically 10 to 20 procedures).

Procedural Risks Remain

While VR training, 3D HD vision, and motion scaling improve accuracy and reduce mechanical errors, baseline surgical risks—such as bleeding, infection, and tissue injury—still exist in every procedure.

The Bottom Line

Surgical robots are not autonomous medical machines, nor are they simple remote arms. They are sophisticated master-slave ecosystems that combine micro-actuation mechanics, high-definition stereo optics, and real-time digital signal processing. By scaling movements, deleting hand tremors, and providing 7 degrees of wrist freedom, these ₹1 Cr+ systems give human surgeons the ability to work inside tiny spaces with microscopic precision.

Frequently asked questions

Does a surgical robot perform operations on its own?

No. Surgical robots are completely human-controlled master-slave telemanipulators. They have no autonomous decision-making capability. The system reads the surgeon's physical hand inputs at the master console, cleans the data through signal processing algorithms, and mirrors those exact movements inside the patient in real time. An attending human surgeon must drive every movement.

How do surgical robots eliminate hand tremors during surgery?

Human physiological hand tremors naturally occur within a frequency band of 6 Hz to 10 Hz. The robot's central microprocessors continuously sample controller coordinates thousands of times per second. Active Digital Signal Processing (DSP) algorithms isolate the 6–10 Hz frequency spectrum and remove these high-frequency vibrations before sending movement commands to the micro-actuators.

Why do surgical robot instruments lock after 10 to 20 uses?

Micro-instruments use tiny tungsten-braided cables and miniaturized wrist joints that experience mechanical stress during operation. To maintain structural safety and prevent cable snapping or joint slippage inside a patient, instruments contain embedded microchips. These chips track usage cycles and automatically lock the tool after a fixed limit, usually 10 to 20 uses.

What is the main mechanical advantage of wristed robotic tools over standard tools?

Standard laparoscopic tools act like rigid chopsticks, offering only 4 degrees of freedom (moving up/down, left/right, in/out, and rotation). Wristed robotic instruments provide 7 degrees of freedom, including pitch, yaw, roll, and elevation. This additional dexterity allows instrument tips to turn corners and work in tight anatomical spaces.

How much do surgical robot systems cost in India?

Capital costs for imported surgical robot platforms generally range from ₹1 Crore up to ₹25+ Crore, depending on features, model configuration, and import duties. However, indigenous Indian platforms like SSI Mantra have entered the market at around ₹4 to ₹5 Crore, aiming to make robotic surgery accessible in tier-2 and tier-3 cities.
